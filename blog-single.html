Three weeks at the Konga washing station

In February we spent three weeks with the Worka cooperative in southern Ethiopia. What we learned about anaerobic fermentation, raised beds, and why our most famous bag tastes the way it does.

Marisol Halvorsen April 12, 2026 14 min read

The road from Addis Ababa to Yirgacheffe is six hundred kilometres long and takes nine hours, eleven if you stop for lunch in Awasa. The last hour is on a dirt track that climbs into the highlands. By the time we arrived at the Konga washing station — at two thousand fifty metres above sea level, in a clearing surrounded by enset trees and coffee shrubs — it was nearly dark, and the day's pickings had been brought down from the surrounding farms in green plastic baskets, ready for sorting.

I have been visiting this region since 2019, but this was our first three-week stay. We came for two reasons. The first was to taste this season's lots before they shipped to us — Konga Natural, Konga Anaerobic, and a small experimental washed lot we had never seen before. The second reason was to watch every stage of the process from the moment a cherry came off the tree until the moment it was packed for export. We had never done it. After eight years, it felt overdue.

The fermentation tank

The first thing you notice at Konga is the smell. It is unmistakable, sweet and slightly fermented and faintly floral, and it hangs over the entire compound. It comes from the open-air fermentation tanks where freshly depulped beans sit in their own mucilage for somewhere between thirty-six and seventy-two hours.

Fermentation is the part of coffee processing that nobody talks about and everybody fights about. Get it right and the cup is bright, complex, alive with fruit and floral notes. Get it wrong and the coffee tastes muddy or sharp or vinegary. The window is small. The tools are mostly experience and a thermometer.

Specialty coffee is a series of small decisions, made in the right order, by people who care. — Endale Worku, Manager, Konga Washing Station

At Konga, Endale and his team taste the fermenting beans every six hours. They have a notebook with hand-drawn graphs. They can tell, from a smell, when the tank is fifteen minutes from being too far gone. None of this is written down anywhere accessible. It is institutional knowledge — the kind that takes a generation to build and a year of indifference to lose.

The anaerobic experiment

The most interesting part of our trip was the anaerobic fermentation tanks. These are sealed stainless-steel containers, about the size of a kitchen oven, where coffee cherries ferment without oxygen for ninety-six hours. The result is a coffee that tastes like nothing else in our catalogue — strawberry jam, lavender, brown sugar, and an almost wine-like depth.

We have been buying small lots of Konga Anaerobic since 2022. This year, we agreed to take their entire production for the seventh batch. Two hundred bags. Limited, expensive, and unlike anything else we will sell in 2026.

What this means for the cup

Coffee processing matters more than most drinkers realise. The same bean, processed three different ways, can taste like three completely different coffees. At Konga we tasted the same daily picking processed as a natural, a washed, and an anaerobic. Three cups, side by side. They shared almost nothing.

Here is what changes:

  • Natural: Cherries dry whole, with the fruit still on the bean. The bean absorbs sugar from the cherry as it dries. Result: heavier body, more fruit-forward, more sweetness.
  • Washed: Cherries are depulped first, then fermented, then washed clean. The bean dries naked. Result: cleaner, brighter, more transparent — you taste the bean, not the fruit.
  • Anaerobic: Cherries ferment in sealed tanks without oxygen, then dry as natural. Result: wine-like, complex, sometimes floral, sometimes fruity, always intense.

"You can taste a place in a coffee. Or you can taste a process. Most coffee in the world is processed to remove the place — to make it consistent, predictable, scalable. We do the opposite."

The economics

Coffee is one of the most volatile commodities in the world. The C-market price — the global benchmark — has swung between $1.20 and $4.20 a pound in the last three years. Most farmers in the world receive a small fraction of that. Most coffee in the world is grown by people who lose money in any given year.

Direct trade is meant to fix this, but the term has been so widely abused that it now means almost nothing. There are companies that call themselves direct trade because they buy from a single broker. We pay between forty and eighty percent above the C-market price for our beans, depending on cup quality and our relationship with the farm. We sign two-year agreements. We pre-finance harvests. We visit twice a year.

None of this is heroic. It is the bare minimum required for the system to be sustainable. But it is also more than what nearly everyone else does, and that should embarrass the industry, not us.

What we are bringing back

Three weeks at Konga changed how we will sell their coffee for the next year. We are doing two things differently. The first is that every bag of Konga Natural will ship with a hand-stamped tag bearing the lot number and the day it was picked — the same way Endale tracks it on the farm. The second is that we are dedicating a full page of our website, and a section of every cafe, to explaining what fermentation is and why it matters.

We think about specialty coffee as a kind of literacy project. The more you know about how a bean got to your cup, the more you can taste in it. The more you taste, the more you care. And the more people care, the more sustainable this entire fragile system becomes.

We will be back at Konga in October.
